1. Why should these methods be connected?

This note started with a small moment of recognition. I was looking at the gradient of on-policy distillation (OPD): sample an output from the student, compare the teacher's and student's log probabilities, and use that difference to weight the gradient of the student's log probability. Then it clicked: this is the structure of policy gradient.

There was a second connection waiting nearby. In variational score distillation (VSD) and distribution matching distillation (DMD), the current scene particles or generator also produce the samples used to improve themselves. But the feedback is a difference of scores, not log probabilities. Since a score is the gradient of a log density with respect to the sample, the natural next question was:

Is the score difference simply the derivative of the reward that appeared in distillation?

Yes—under the right assumptions. That is the exciting part: the connection is an algebraic identity, and we can follow it all the way from the objective to the update. The resemblance in the training loops was a clue; the density ratio explains it.

The methods initially feel far apart because they solve different practical problems. In language-model RL, we may have a verifier that can judge an answer but cannot tell us which token to change. In distillation, we have a teacher that can evaluate what the student actually says. In diffusion distillation, we can differentiate a generated image through its generator, but usually cannot evaluate the generator's output density. Each setting exposes a different handle on the same underlying object: a distribution we want to improve.

Consider the teaching problem behind OPD. A student trained only on polished teacher answers may encounter prefixes at inference time that never appeared in its training examples. Asking the teacher to respond to the student's own attempts addresses that mismatch. This is the motivation for on-policy data in GKD; the choice of divergence remains a separate decision. [2] Once we choose reverse KL, the teacher does more than provide a desired answer: it tells us which outputs the student underweights or overweights.

Now consider an image generator. There need not be one correct image for each latent input. What matters can be the distribution of all its outputs. The teacher's feedback must then describe that distribution, rather than assign a target image to every latent. A score difference supplies exactly the local directional information needed to update a differentiable generator.

Two more connections make the story richer. If a diffusion step has a Gaussian transition with fixed covariance, we can average its policy gradient analytically: the result is a squared-error gradient between student and teacher means. If neither density is available, a GAN discriminator offers another route. It learns to tell target samples from student samples; at its ideal optimum, its logit recovers their log-density ratio. Backpropagating through that scalar field gives a score difference. We will derive both connections, while keeping local transition matching separate from matching an entire output population.

To connect the methods, keep three questions separate: What distribution are we optimizing? What objective defines improvement? What derivative can we actually compute? We will change one answer at a time. First, a teacher becomes a reward. Next, a reward becomes a target distribution. Finally, differentiating that reward in sample space turns it into the two-score update.

Notation: \(q_\theta\) is the student, \(p_T\) the teacher, and \(p\) a fixed target. A prompt is held fixed; we may subsequently average over a fixed prompt distribution. Integrals become sums for discrete outputs. For each KL calculation, assume \(q_\theta\ll p\), finite required expectations, and differentiability on parameter-independent support with derivatives that can pass through the integrals. Here \(q_\theta\ll p\) means the student puts no mass where the target has zero mass. The pathwise identities additionally require smooth sample-space densities and a differentiable sampler whose base randomness is independent of \(\theta\). Density scores are \(s_q=\nabla_x\log q_\theta\) and \(s_p=\nabla_x\log p\).

2. RL: improve the policy through a scalar reward

Start with a complete output \(x\), a policy \(q_\theta\), and a reward \(r(x)\) that is fixed with respect to \(\theta\). RL optimizes the policy to increase expected reward; it does not, in this step, optimize the reward function itself.

\[ J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{x\sim q_\theta}[r(x)]. \] \[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta J &=\int r(x)\,\nabla_\theta q_\theta(x)\,dx\\ &=\mathbb E_{q_\theta}\!\left[r(x)\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta(x)\right]. \end{aligned} \] (1) The likelihood-ratio / REINFORCE identity

This is the REINFORCE structure. [1] We do not need \(\nabla_x r\): a scalar judgment is enough. This is useful for discrete tokens, environment interactions, and nondifferentiable verifiers. For a fixed prompt, a baseline independent of the sampled output leaves the expected gradient unchanged because \(\mathbb E_{q_\theta}[\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta]=0\). In a trajectory, use the corresponding conditional identity at each state. Baselines are held fixed in the policy-gradient backward pass. Positive weights favor an output's log probability in that gradient contribution; shared parameters and the other samples determine its net change.

3. OPD: a teacher supplies an implicit reward

Choose a sequence-level reverse-KL distillation objective:

\[ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{OPD}}(\theta) =\mathrm{KL}(q_\theta\|p_T) =\int q_\theta(x)\log\frac{q_\theta(x)}{p_T(x)}\,dx. \] \[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\mathrm{OPD}} &=\int \nabla_\theta q_\theta(x) \left(\log\frac{q_\theta(x)}{p_T(x)}+1\right)dx\\ &=\mathbb E_{q_\theta}\!\left[ (\log q_\theta-\log p_T)\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta\right]. \end{aligned} \] (2) The +1 integrates to zero: \(\nabla_\theta\int q_\theta\,dx=0\)

Negate the loss to align with RL's maximization convention:

\[ \boxed{\nabla_\theta(-\mathcal L_{\mathrm{OPD}}) =\mathbb E_{q_\theta}\!\left[ \underbrace{\log p_T(x)-\log q_\theta(x)}_{r_{\rm implicit}(x)} \nabla_\theta\log q_\theta(x)\right].} \] (3) A teacher–student log ratio is a policy-gradient reward

The reward asks a relative question: does the teacher assign this output more probability than the student does? A plausible output can still be overproduced by the student. The subtraction of \(\log q_\theta\) detects that. This sequence-level reverse-KL and policy-gradient connection is explicit in MiniLLM. [3]

The implicit reward depends on \(\theta\). Usually a parameter-dependent reward adds an extra derivative. Here that term is \(-\mathbb E_{q_\theta}[\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta]=0\), with the derivative taken at fixed \(x\). This cancellation holds in expectation under the current student, not sample by sample. It justifies treating the log ratio as a constant weight in the sampled surrogate for (3), not ignoring reward derivatives in general.

Sequences need credit assignment

Use a fixed horizon \(H\), with EOS represented as an absorbing terminal state if generation ends earlier. Include its stopping probability in the sequence likelihood. Let \(h_k\) contain the prompt and preceding tokens. Define the per-token log ratio \(r_k\) and its reward-to-go \(R_k\):

\[ r_k=\log\frac{p_T(y_k\mid h_k)}{q_\theta(y_k\mid h_k)}, \qquad R_k=\sum_{j=k}^{H}r_j. \] \[ \nabla_\theta(-\mathcal L_{\mathrm{OPD}}) =\mathbb E\!\left[\sum_{k=1}^{H} (R_k-b(h_k))\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta(y_k\mid h_k)\right]. \] (4) Earlier actions can affect later distillation rewards

The causal reward-to-go follows by removing past rewards whose expected contribution is zero. Using only the immediate \(r_k\) generally loses the effect of an earlier token on later prefixes. MiniLLM's decomposition makes this future-reward term visible. [3] PPO adds a surrogate with old/new policy ratios and clipping on top of policy-gradient reasoning; clipping does not follow automatically from differentiating KL. [4]

“On-policy” specifies where data come from, not a unique loss OPD can use several divergences. GKD, for example, evaluates token-distribution losses on student-generated prefixes while stopping gradients through prefix sampling. [2] Its reverse-KL variant should not be identified with the full sequence gradient in (4): the latter also accounts for how parameters change which prefixes are visited. The exact equivalence here concerns sequence-level reverse KL, or a single fixed-context action distribution.
Exactly what does a frozen-prefix token loss omit?

Let \(d_{\theta,k}(h)\) be the student's prefix distribution at step \(k\), and define \(D_k(\theta,h)=\mathrm{KL}(q_\theta(\cdot\mid h)\|p_T(\cdot\mid h))\). For the fixed horizon, the KL chain rule gives

\[ \mathcal L_{\rm seq}=\sum_{k=1}^{H}\mathbb E_{h\sim d_{\theta,k}}[D_k(\theta,h)], \] \[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\rm seq} ={}&\underbrace{\sum_k\mathbb E_{d_{\theta,k}}[\nabla_\theta D_k]}_{\text{differentiate the token KL at a fixed prefix}}\\ &+\underbrace{\sum_k\mathbb E_{d_{\theta,k}}[D_k\nabla_\theta\log d_{\theta,k}]}_{\text{differentiate which prefixes are visited}}. \end{aligned} \] (4a) Equal objective values do not imply equal gradients after stop-gradient

A two-step example makes the missing term concrete. Let the student's first token choose branch B with probability \(u=\operatorname{sigmoid}(\eta)\); the teacher chooses it with probability \(1/2\). At the second step the student's fixed probability of token 1 is \(1/2\) on both branches. The teacher's is \(1/2\) on A and \(0.9\) on B. Then \(D_A=0\), \(D_B=\tfrac12\log(25/9)\approx0.5108\), and

\[ \mathcal L_{\rm seq} =\mathrm{KL}(\operatorname{Ber}(u)\|\operatorname{Ber}(1/2))+uD_B, \] \[ \left.\partial_\eta\mathcal L_{\rm seq}\right|_{\eta=0} =\tfrac14D_B\approx0.1277, \qquad g_{\rm frozen\ prefix}=0. \]

The full gradient can change how often the student visits the branch it cannot match. The fixed-prefix loss has no such signal for \(\eta\). Equation (4a) isolates this distinction; GKD's actual recipe also allows data mixing, other divergences, and per-sequence length normalization. [2] Reproduce this example and the gradient checks.

4. KL-regularized RL defines a target distribution

So far, distillation has acquired an RL interpretation. The connection also works in the other direction. Let \(p_{\rm ref}\) and \(r\) be fixed, \(\beta>0\), and restrict to policies with finite \(\mathrm{KL}(q_\theta\|p_{\rm ref})\) and finite expected absolute reward. Consider

\[ J_\beta(\theta)=\mathbb E_{q_\theta}[r(x)] -\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(q_\theta\|p_{\rm ref}). \] \[ p^*(x)=\frac{p_{\rm ref}(x)e^{r(x)/\beta}}{Z}, \qquad Z=\int p_{\rm ref}(x)e^{r(x)/\beta}\,dx. \] (5) Assume \(0<Z<\infty\)

The reference supplies a starting distribution; exponentiated reward tilts it toward preferred outputs. Substitute \(\log p^*=\log p_{\rm ref}+r/\beta-\log Z\) into reverse KL:

\[ \begin{aligned} \beta\,\mathrm{KL}(q_\theta\|p^*) &=\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(q_\theta\|p_{\rm ref}) -\mathbb E_{q_\theta}[r]+\beta\log Z,\\[2pt] \boxed{J_\beta(\theta)} &\boxed{=-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(q_\theta\|p^*)+\beta\log Z.} \end{aligned} \] (6) An exact objective identity

Because \(Z\) is independent of the student parameters, the two optimization problems have the same optimizers. If \(p_T=p^*\), sequence-level reverse-KL distillation has the same objective up to scale and a constant. A capacity-limited student's global optimum, if attained, is its best available reverse-KL fit; gradient training is not guaranteed to find it.

This reward–optimal-policy transformation also underlies DPO's derivation. [5] The identity alone does not give DPO's preference-pair loss, which additionally uses a preference model. Nor does an arbitrary teacher necessarily equal the optimum of a particular externally chosen reward.

Two boundaries of the equivalence: plain RL and general environments

Plain expected-reward maximization lacks the entropy/reference term in (6). For a finite action space, adding entropy corresponds to using a uniform reference. In a continuous unbounded space, a “uniform reference everywhere” is not a normalized distribution. We should therefore say KL-regularized RL when claiming this exact equivalence.

The algebra also holds for trajectory distributions with a common fixed environment. However, the reward-tilted trajectory distribution need not be realizable by a policy with those same stochastic dynamics. The objective identity remains true over feasible policies; the unconstrained optimum need not be attainable. For a fixed prompt and autoregressive text, we can simply treat the whole response as \(x\).

5. Gaussian OPD: policy gradient becomes mean MSE

Here is a particularly satisfying specialization: for a Gaussian policy and teacher with the same fixed covariance, the expected log-ratio policy gradient is exactly a mean-matching gradient. Continuity alone does not imply MSE; the Gaussian assumptions do the work.

First keep the cost and reward signs consistent

Keep this note's notation: \(q_\theta\) is the student and \(p_T\) the teacher. Define the cost \(c_\theta(a)=\log q_\theta(a)-\log p_T(a)=-r_\theta(a)\). With a fixed teacher, \(\partial_\theta c_\theta|_a=\partial_\theta\log q_\theta|_a\). Reading the reverse-KL derivation backwards gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E_{q_\theta}[c_\theta\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta] &=\int (\nabla_\theta q_\theta)c_\theta\,da +\underbrace{\int q_\theta\,\partial_\theta c_\theta\,da}_{0}\\ &=\nabla_\theta\int q_\theta c_\theta\,da =\nabla_\theta\mathrm{KL}(q_\theta\|p_T). \end{aligned} \] (6a) Positive student/teacher log ratio → loss gradient; negate it for reward ascent

This adds a zero expectation and then uses the product rule. The derivatives in the integral hold the integration variable \(a\) fixed. Once we substitute a differentiable sampled action, total derivatives have an additional path through that action.

Integrate out the Gaussian action noise

Hold a context \(h\) fixed and suppress it in the notation. Let \(\sigma>0\) be independent of \(\theta\), and let the teacher mean be fixed at this context:

\[ q_\theta(a)=\mathcal N(\mu_\theta,\sigma^2I), \qquad p_T(a)=\mathcal N(\mu_T,\sigma^2I), \] \[ \delta=\mu_\theta-\mu_T,\quad a=\mu_\theta+\sigma\epsilon,\quad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I). \]

The sampled cost has a constant part and a part correlated with the sampled noise:

\[ \begin{aligned} c_\theta(a) &=\frac{\|a-\mu_T\|^2-\|a-\mu_\theta\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\\ &=\underbrace{\frac{\|\delta\|^2}{2\sigma^2}}_{C_\theta} +\frac{\delta^{\mathsf T}\epsilon}{\sigma},\\ \left.\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta(a)\right|_a &=J_\mu^{\mathsf T}\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma}, \qquad J_\mu=\frac{\partial\mu_\theta}{\partial\theta}. \end{aligned} \]

Multiply them and use \(\mathbb E[\epsilon]=0\), \(\mathbb E[\epsilon\epsilon^{\mathsf T}]=I\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[c_\theta\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta] &=J_\mu^{\mathsf T}\!\left( \frac{C_\theta}{\sigma}\mathbb E[\epsilon] +\frac{\mathbb E[\epsilon\epsilon^{\mathsf T}]\delta}{\sigma^2}\right)\\ &=\boxed{J_\mu^{\mathsf T}\frac{\mu_\theta-\mu_T}{\sigma^2}}\\ &=\boxed{\nabla_\theta\frac{\|\mu_\theta-\mu_T\|^2}{2\sigma^2}}. \end{aligned} \] (6b) Expected policy gradient = analytic Gaussian KL gradient = scaled mean-MSE gradient

The entire action expectation has been evaluated. The mean gradient now has no variance from sampling \(\epsilon\) at this fixed context; sampling prompts, states, or noise levels can still contribute variance. A shared fixed covariance \(\Sigma\succ0\) gives the more general Mahalanobis loss \(\tfrac12\delta^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}\delta\). At this fixed covariance, the two directions of Gaussian KL even coincide. A trainable covariance adds covariance and entropy derivatives.

A sampled log ratio is not an already-averaged KL reward The correlation between \(c_\theta(a)\) and \(\epsilon\) supplies the gradient in (6b). Replacing \(c_\theta(a)\) by its mean \(C_\theta\) and detaching it gives \(\mathbb E[C_\theta\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta(a)]=0\) at fixed \(h\). Directly differentiating \(C_\theta\), by contrast, gives the desired MSE gradient. These are different operations; clipping does not establish an equivalence between them.

Flow-OPD and DiffusionOPD: which distribution is Gaussian?

In a discretized diffusion SDE, the Gaussian can be the next-state transition conditioned on the current state. Both models are evaluated at the same student-visited state. DiffusionOPD derives the shared-covariance KL and uses direct transition matching; Flow-OPD also derives a Gaussian KL but describes a clipped policy update with detached mean-matching rewards. Their published v1 recipes are distinct. [10] [11] Equation (6b) establishes the local sampled-log-ratio identity, rather than an equivalence to every PPO surrogate in those papers.

For example, write a transition mean as \(\mu_\theta(h,t)=b(h,t)+k(t)v_\theta(h,t)\), with the same fixed \(b,k,\sigma_t\) for both models. Then

\[ \mathrm{KL}\big(q_\theta(\cdot\mid h,t)\|p_T(\cdot\mid h,t)\big) =\frac{k(t)^2}{2\sigma_t^2}\|v_\theta(h,t)-v_T(h,t)\|^2. \] (6c) Matching transition means becomes a weighted prediction MSE

A simple noisy Euler step has \(b(h,t)=h\) and \(k(t)=\Delta t\); an SDE conversion can change both coefficients. This is a conditional transition loss, not the KL between the final generated-image marginals. Moreover, differentiating it on detached rollout states gives a local update. The full trajectory objective also differentiates which states are visited, exactly as in (4a); Gaussian integration removes action noise, not that state-distribution derivative.

What changes for a deterministic ODE sampler?

At a fixed state, a deterministic step is a point mass at its predicted next state. Two distinct point masses have infinite KL, not a finite MSE. Introducing a common positive Gaussian observation noise makes the transition KL above well-defined; directly regressing the two deterministic predictions is another valid choice. DiffusionOPD explicitly replaces the KL with squared transition error in its ODE formulation, Eq. (12). [10]

For fixed unequal means, the Gaussian KL diverges as \(\sigma\to0\); the rescaled quantity \(\sigma^2\mathrm{KL}=\tfrac12\|\delta\|^2\) stays finite. This explains the regression connection without claiming a finite zero-noise KL. Separately, an ODE driven by a random initial latent can have a smooth, non-Gaussian final marginal. Deterministic transitions do not make the final population deterministic or Gaussian.

6. From rewarding samples to moving samples

Now suppose the sample is continuous and generated by a differentiable map:

\[ x=G_\theta(z),\qquad z\sim p_z, \qquad J_\theta(z)=\frac{\partial G_\theta(z)}{\partial\theta}. \]

The latent distribution \(p_z\) is fixed. Instead of differentiating the probability of a sampled output, we can hold \(z\) fixed and differentiate the output itself. For this section, assume \(q_\theta\) really has a smooth density; differentiability of \(G_\theta\) alone does not ensure that. Reparameterize the same reverse KL, with \(p\) fixed:

\[ \mathcal L(\theta)=\mathbb E_z\!\left[ \log q_\theta(G_\theta(z))-\log p(G_\theta(z))\right]. \] \[ \nabla_\theta\mathcal L =\mathbb E_z\!\left[J_\theta^{\mathsf T}(s_q-s_p)\right] +\underbrace{\mathbb E_{x\sim q_\theta}\!\left[ \left.\partial_\theta\log q_\theta(x)\right|_x\right]}_{0}. \] (7) Separate movement through x from explicit density dependence

Again, normalization cancels the explicit density-parameter derivative in expectation. What remains is the score difference, pulled back through the generator Jacobian:

\[ \boxed{\nabla_\theta(-\mathcal L) =\mathbb E_z\!\left[J_\theta^{\mathsf T}(s_p-s_q)\right].} \] \[ \boxed{\nabla_x\underbrace{(\log p-\log q_\theta)}_{\text{implicit reward}} =\underbrace{s_p-s_q}_{\text{sample-space direction}}.} \] (8) The bridge from OPD to score distillation

This answers the opening question. At fixed \(\theta\), the log ratio grades an output; its spatial derivative describes how that grade changes as \(x\) moves. For the same smooth continuous model and target, (3) and (8) have the same expected parameter gradient. Their sampled estimates and variance differ. This does not differentiate through sampled token IDs: discrete language OPD and continuous image distillation instantiate the calculus on different output spaces. Also distinguish the parameter score \(\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta\) in REINFORCE from the sample-space density score \(\nabla_x\log q_\theta\) in diffusion.

A Gaussian check: the two estimators really give the same gradient

Let \(q_\mu=\mathcal N(\mu,1)\), \(p=\mathcal N(m,1)\), and \(x=\mu+z\), with \(z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\). The exact loss is \(\mathcal L=\tfrac12(\mu-m)^2\), so its negative gradient is \(m-\mu\).

\[ r_\mu(x)=(m-\mu)x+\tfrac12(\mu^2-m^2), \qquad \partial_\mu\log q_\mu(x)=x-\mu. \] \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Likelihood ratio:}\quad &\mathbb E[r_\mu(x)(x-\mu)]=m-\mu,\\ \text{Pathwise:}\quad &\mathbb E[\underbrace{\partial_\mu x}_{1} \underbrace{(s_p-s_q)}_{m-\mu}]=m-\mu. \end{aligned} \]

For Figure 2, both give \(+2\). The pathwise estimator happens to have zero variance in this mean-only example; that is a property of this Gaussian calculation, not a universal variance guarantee.

To check the entropy contribution too, allow the scale to change: \(q_{\mu,\ell}=\mathcal N(\mu,e^{2\ell})\), \(p=\mathcal N(m,v^2)\). Both estimators give the ascent gradient

\[ \nabla_{(\mu,\ell)}(-\mathcal L) =\left(\frac{m-\mu}{v^2},\;1-\frac{e^{2\ell}}{v^2}\right). \]

The \(+1\) in the scale component comes from student entropy. The numerical checks compare both components against the closed form and finite differences.

The score difference is a direction in output space. An actual generator update is \(\Delta\theta\propto\mathbb E[J_\theta^{\mathsf T}(s_p-s_q)]\), so shared parameters couple the movement of different outputs. It is not generally identical to moving every output independently along a free particle flow. The previous flow note develops that distinction.

7. VSD and DMD: make the comparison at noise scales

The clean-space derivation leaves a practical problem. A neural generator often has no tractable likelihood; a singular clean distribution does not even have the density used in (7). We can instead define the objective directly at positive noise levels. In the ideal model, \(p_T\) denotes a target clean probability law and \(s_{T,t}\) its exact forward-noised score. A trained diffusion network approximates this score. Apply the same Gaussian kernel to both clean laws, with \(t,z,\epsilon\) sampled independently from fixed distributions:

\[ x_t=\alpha_tG_\theta(z)+\sigma_t\epsilon, \qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I), \] \[ K_t(\cdot\mid x_0)=\mathcal N(\alpha_t x_0,\sigma_t^2I), \] \[ \begin{aligned} q_{\theta,t}(x_t)&=\int K_t(x_t\mid x_0)\,q_\theta(dx_0),\\ p_{T,t}(x_t)&=\int K_t(x_t\mid x_0)\,p_T(dx_0). \end{aligned} \] \[ \boxed{\mathcal L_{\rm noisy}(\theta) =\mathbb E_{t\sim\rho}\!\left[ w(t)\,\mathrm{KL}(q_{\theta,t}\|p_{T,t})\right].} \] (9) Fixed \(\rho\), nonnegative \(w(t)\), and \(\sigma_t>0\) at sampled noise levels

The measure notation \(q_\theta(dx_0)\) includes singular laws such as a point mass. A nondegenerate Gaussian kernel gives smooth, positive noisy densities, but does not by itself guarantee a finite KL or finite time-integrated gradient; the integrability assumptions still matter, especially near \(\sigma_t=0\).

These are noisy marginals of the final output distribution. We integrate out the clean image and noise; a marginal describes the resulting population at a chosen noise level. It need not describe an intermediate state visited by the student's actual sampler. Even a one-step student can be evaluated at many such levels. Training samples levels from \(\rho\), rather than evaluating every level on every step. Replacing clean KL by (9) changes the objective; reparameterization changes how that new objective is differentiated.

Apply the pathwise argument to the smooth noisy law at each \(t\), without differentiating a possibly nonexistent clean density. The schedule is fixed, so \(\partial_\theta x_t=\alpha_tJ_\theta\). With \(s_{q,t}=\nabla_{x_t}\log q_{\theta,t}\) and \(s_{T,t}=\nabla_{x_t}\log p_{T,t}\),

\[ \boxed{\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\rm noisy} =\mathbb E_{t,z,\epsilon}\!\left[ w(t)\alpha_tJ_\theta^{\mathsf T} \big(s_{q,t}(x_t)-s_{T,t}(x_t)\big)\right].} \] (10) Loss gradient; gradient descent uses its negative

This is the ideal noisy-marginal two-score gradient. Diff-Instruct explicitly formulates an integral KL of this kind. [7] The original DMD derives a clean-distribution score gradient and implements it through noisy scores, with practical weighting and an additional regression term. [8] Equation (9) captures the shared mathematical core with fixed time weights; it is not a verbatim specification of every DMD training loss.

Where VSD fits

VSD's original setting is text-to-3D. It optimizes a distribution \(\mu\) over scene parameters. For a camera \(c\), rendering \(x_0=R(\vartheta,c)\), \(\vartheta\sim\mu\), induces \(q_t^\mu(\cdot\mid c)\). Its noisy-image KL is averaged over cameras and noise levels, with a view-conditioned teacher target. The paper derives a Wasserstein gradient flow in scene-parameter distribution space and approximates it with particles; each particle receives a score difference through its renderer Jacobian. [6]

DMD instead trains a shared image generator \(G_\theta\). [8] Thus the connection to VSD is through the noisy-KL first variation and the resulting two-score field; VSD is not simply the same finite-dimensional reparameterization optimizer. Both scene representation and generator capacity constrain the distributions that can be matched.

Why there is a fake-score network

The teacher estimates \(s_{T,t}\). The other score must describe the current student's population. Train an auxiliary denoiser on newly generated images with the generator held fixed:

\[ \min_\phi\;\mathbb E_{t,z,\epsilon} \left[\lambda(t)\|\epsilon_\phi(x_t,t)-\epsilon\|^2\right]. \] \[ \epsilon_q^*(x_t,t)=\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_t,t], \qquad s_{q,t}(x_t)=-\frac{\epsilon_q^*(x_t,t)}{\sigma_t}. \] (11) Positive denoising weights on the trained noise levels

The conditional mean is the optimum over all square-integrable prediction functions; a finite neural network may not represent or reach it. For conditional generation, condition on the prompt—and on the camera in VSD—throughout the denoising problem. In particular, the individual sampled noise \(\epsilon\) is not generally the marginal predictor \(\epsilon_q^*\).

Let \(\epsilon_T^*=-\sigma_t s_{T,t}\). Substituting exact predictors makes the sign explicit:

\[ \nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\rm noisy} =\mathbb E\!\left[\frac{w(t)\alpha_t}{\sigma_t} J_\theta^{\mathsf T}(\epsilon_T^*-\epsilon_q^*)\right], \] \[ \Delta\theta=-\eta\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\rm noisy}. \] (11a) Teacher minus fake predicts the loss gradient; the descent update reverses it

Training uses learned \(\epsilon_\phi\) and teacher estimates in place of these ideal predictors. Finite model capacity, optimization error, and a fake model lagging behind the student can bias the resulting gradient. [6] [7] [8]

The same entropy term appears in two forms When the terms are separately finite, \(-\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)=\mathbb E_q[\log p]+H(q)\), with differential entropy for continuous densities. In the likelihood-ratio view, entropy contributes \(-\log q\); in the pathwise ascent gradient, it contributes \(-\mathbb E[J_\theta^{\mathsf T}s_q]\). This term matters when student entropy can change. For a fixed-variance Gaussian with only its mean trainable, entropy is constant and its expected gradient is zero. Also, this is output-distribution entropy, not VSD's scene-parameter entropy; it does not guarantee mode coverage.

8. GANs: learn the ratio, then differentiate it

There is another way to obtain the field that moves generated samples. Suppose we can sample from the target \(p\) and the current student \(q_\theta\), but cannot evaluate either density. Train a binary discriminator \(D_\psi(x)\) to classify target samples as real and student samples as fake, with equal class weights:

\[ \max_\psi\; \mathbb E_p[\log D_\psi(x)] +\mathbb E_{q_\theta}[\log(1-D_\psi(x))]. \] \[ D^*(x)=\frac{p(x)}{p(x)+q_\theta(x)}. \] (12) The ideal logistic discriminator at a fixed student

This is the optimal discriminator in the original GAN analysis. [12] Its probability output is not a log-density ratio. Its logit is:

\[ f^*(x)=\operatorname{logit}D^*(x) =\log\frac{D^*(x)}{1-D^*(x)} =\log p(x)-\log q_\theta(x), \] \[ \boxed{\nabla_x f^*(x)=s_p(x)-s_q(x).} \] (13) Learn a scalar density ratio; differentiate it to obtain the score difference

This gives two ways of estimating the same ideal field: diffusion denoisers estimate the two scores separately, while a discriminator logit estimates their log-density ratio jointly. Its input gradient estimates the difference of scores, not either individual score. For noisy marginals, train \(D_\psi(x_t,t)\) on target and student samples corrupted with the same kernel and time distribution.

In Figure 2's Gaussian example, \(D^*(x)=\operatorname{sigmoid}(2x)\). Its logit is \(2x\), whose derivative is \(+2\): exactly the rightward field already drawn in the figure.

Which generator loss recovers reverse KL?

At the current student \(\theta_0\), suppose \(f_{\psi_0}=\log p-\log q_{\theta_0}\). Hold this fitted function fixed while differentiating through \(x=G_\theta(z)\). Choose the generator loss

\[ \mathcal L_G(\theta;\psi_0)=-\mathbb E_z[f_{\psi_0}(G_\theta(z))]. \] \[ \left.\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_G\right|_{\theta_0} =-\mathbb E_z[J_{\theta_0}^{\mathsf T}\nabla_x f_{\psi_0}] =\left.\nabla_\theta\mathrm{KL}(q_\theta\|p)\right|_{\theta_0}. \] (14) Negative-logit loss with an ideal, current discriminator matches the reverse-KL gradient locally

The fitted ratio must track the student as it changes. Equality at \(\theta_0\) does not turn a stale discriminator into the exact KL objective for all later parameters. The broader idea of selecting a divergence through adversarial training is developed in f-GAN. [13]

# Generator step: freeze discriminator parameters, keep its input gradient.
discriminator.eval()
for parameter in discriminator.parameters():
    parameter.requires_grad_(False)
x = generator(z)
loss_ratio = -discriminator.logit(x).mean()
loss_ratio.backward()  # backpropagate through discriminator INTO x
# Restore training mode and parameter gradients for the discriminator update.

This differs from the two-score surrogate: there we explicitly compute a vector field and detach it before taking its inner product with the generated sample. Here the network outputs a scalar, so we retain its input derivative to obtain the field. Detaching the logit would remove the generator's training signal.

Why standard GAN losses are not all reverse KL

Write \(D(x)=\operatorname{sigmoid}(f(x))\). With discriminator parameters fixed, the negative sample-space gradients of three generator losses are

Generator loss \(\ell(x)\)Descent field \(-\nabla_x\ell\)
Negative logit: \(-f(x)\)\(\nabla_x f(x)\)
Minimax: \(\log(1-D(x))\)\(D(x)\nabla_x f(x)\)
Non-saturating: \(-\log D(x)\)\((1-D(x))\nabla_x f(x)\)

At the ideal discriminator, substitute \(\nabla_x f=s_p-s_q\). All three use this field, but their sample-dependent weights differ. After averaging and multiplying by generator Jacobians, their parameter updates need not be parallel. The original optimized minimax game is \(-\log4+2\mathrm{JS}(p\|q_\theta)\); its non-saturating alternative changes the generator loss. [12] A shared ratio therefore does not make every GAN a reverse-KL optimizer.

The GAN tutorial emphasizes that this view depends on supervised density-ratio estimation. [14] For the derivative identity here, assume smooth positive densities and an ideal classifier. A finite discriminator can classify well while estimating spatial derivatives poorly. With separated supports, logits may diverge; common Gaussian smoothing can restore overlap, but does not guarantee accurate training. As with DMD, the generator Jacobian couples outputs: “particle movement” describes the sample-space field, not independent control of every sample.

9. What survives in an actual training loop?

The exact identities are useful because they tell us where to stop gradients and where an implementation departs from its ideal objective.

A sampled reverse-KL policy-gradient surrogate

# x is sampled from exactly q_theta, with no sampling gradient.
# Sum response-token log probabilities, including EOS; mask padding.
logq = student_log_prob(x)
logp = teacher_log_prob(x)
reward = (logp - logq).detach()
loss_pg = -(reward * logq).mean()
loss_pg.backward()

This illustrates (3), with one sequence log probability per batch element and a shared tokenization/output space for teacher and student. The sampling law must equal the \(q_\theta\) used in the log probabilities. Sampling with temperature, top-k/top-p truncation, or an old policy while evaluating an unmodified current policy does not give this estimator without further changes. Importance correction requires adequate support; truncation may remove it. Dividing by a sampled response's variable length generally changes the objective; a fixed divisor only rescales it.

The log-ratio weight is detached to implement the derived gradient. This does not mean detaching the student distribution inside an explicitly summed token KL. Conversely, directly averaging \(\log q_\theta(x)-\log p_T(x)\) on detached samples and calling backward misses the dependence of the sampling distribution on \(\theta\).

A two-score generator surrogate

# Generator update: both score estimates are evaluated, then detached.
x0 = generator(z)
xt = alpha * x0 + sigma * noise
with no_grad():
    grad_xt = w * (fake_score(xt, t) - teacher_score(xt, t))
loss_dm = (grad_xt * xt).flatten(1).sum(1).mean()
loss_dm.backward()  # d xt / d theta supplies alpha * the generator Jacobian

Here fixed time weights broadcast per sample, and both functions return density scores. The whole computed score difference is detached, including its dependence on \(x_t\); merely freezing the score networks' parameters would not do that. Keep the path through the final \(x_t\) differentiable. A separate denoising update detaches generated images and trains the fake model. The generator surrogate's scalar value is not a KL estimate. By contrast, the raw sampled log ratio is a KL estimate, but needs the correct gradient treatment discussed above.

The teacher target in (9) is an idealized probability law, not automatically the output law of a particular finite-step teacher sampler. Approximate scores, solver discretization, and guidance can separate the two. Even a guided field that is the score of a normalized density at each \(t\) need not describe marginals obtained by forward-noising one common clean law. These are additional assumptions when interpreting an implementation as distillation to \(p_T\).

DMD's weighting is a concrete further difference. Its paper's Eq. (8) uses a factor proportional to \((\sigma_t^2/\alpha_t)/\operatorname{MAE}(\hat x_T(x_t,t),x_0)\), where \(\hat x_T\) is the teacher's denoised prediction. The denominator depends on the generated sample. [8] Treating this factor as a detached weight does not make it a fixed function \(w(t)\), or establish that the applied field is the gradient of (9). The method-by-method note discusses further differences.

The full original DMD objective additionally includes paired regression. [8] DMD2 removes that requirement, improves fake-score tracking with a two-time-scale update scheme, and introduces an adversarial loss using real data. [9] These modifications matter: “DMD is reverse KL plus reparameterization” is a useful description of its distribution-matching core, not its complete recipe.

10. A compact map of the connection

ViewTraining signalUpdate
Policy gradient / discrete OPDExternal reward or teacher–student log ratioWeight \(\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta\) by a scalar return
Gaussian OPDLocal KL with shared fixed covarianceAnalytic mean-MSE gradient
VSD / DMDScores of noisy output distributionsBackpropagate a two-score field
GAN / discriminatorA learned log-density ratioBackpropagate the logit, weighted by the generator loss

The connection can now be stated precisely: KL-regularized RL supplies a target distribution. Reverse-KL OPD uses its log ratio as a policy-gradient signal. At a fixed condition, equal-covariance Gaussians turn that expectation into mean MSE. In continuous sample space, the ratio's derivative gives the two-score field used in score distillation; a discriminator offers another way to estimate it. Discrete outputs do not force PPO, continuous outputs do not force MSE, and a GAN's loss still determines which update it applies.

What made this connection satisfying was that the “reward” and the “direction” had been next to each other all along:

\[ \underbrace{\log p-\log q_\theta}_{\text{how much to favor an output}} \quad\xrightarrow{\quad\nabla_x\quad}\quad \underbrace{s_p-s_q}_{\text{which way to move that output}}. \]

When reading a new method, I can now ask whether it changed the target, the divergence, the sampling distribution, or the gradient estimator. A new-looking training loop often becomes much easier to understand once those choices are visible.
