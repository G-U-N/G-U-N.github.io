1. Why should these methods be connected?

This note started with a small moment of recognition. I was looking at the gradient of on-policy distillation (OPD): sample an output from the student, compare the teacher's and student's log probabilities, and use that difference to weight the gradient of the student's log probability. Then it clicked: this is the structure of policy gradient.

There was a second connection waiting nearby. In variational score distillation (VSD) and distribution matching distillation (DMD), the generator also produces the samples used to improve itself. But the feedback is a difference of scores, not log probabilities. Since a score is the gradient of a log density with respect to the sample, the natural next question was:

Is the score difference simply the derivative of the reward that appeared in distillation?

Yes—under the right assumptions. That is the exciting part: the connection is an algebraic identity, and we can follow it all the way from the objective to the update. The resemblance in the training loops was a clue; the density ratio explains it.

The methods initially feel far apart because they solve different practical problems. In language-model RL, we may have a verifier that can judge an answer but cannot tell us which token to change. In distillation, we have a teacher that can evaluate what the student actually says. In diffusion distillation, we can differentiate a generated image through its generator, but usually cannot evaluate the generator's output density. Each setting exposes a different handle on the same underlying object: a distribution we want to improve.

Consider the teaching problem behind OPD. A student trained only on polished teacher answers may encounter prefixes at inference time that never appeared in its training examples. Asking the teacher to respond to the student's own attempts addresses that mismatch. This is the motivation for on-policy data in GKD; the choice of divergence remains a separate decision. [2] Once we choose reverse KL, the teacher does more than provide a desired answer: it tells us which outputs the student underweights or overweights.

Now consider an image generator. There need not be one correct image for each latent input. What matters can be the distribution of all its outputs. The teacher's feedback must then describe that distribution, rather than assign a target image to every latent. A score difference supplies exactly the local directional information needed to update a differentiable generator.

To connect the methods, keep three questions separate: What distribution are we optimizing? What objective defines improvement? What derivative can we actually compute? We will change one answer at a time. First, a teacher becomes a reward. Next, a reward becomes a target distribution. Finally, differentiating that reward in sample space turns it into the two-score update.

Notation: \(q_\theta\) is the student, \(p_T\) the teacher, and \(p\) a fixed target. A prompt or condition is held fixed and suppressed; averaging over a fixed prompt distribution changes none of the identities. Density scores are \(s_q=\nabla_x\log q_\theta\) and \(s_p=\nabla_x\log p\). We assume the required expectations are finite, differentiation can pass under the integral, and the relevant densities have compatible, parameter-independent support. The pathwise sections additionally assume smooth densities and a differentiable sampler.

2. RL: improve the policy through a scalar reward

Start with a complete output \(x\), a policy \(q_\theta\), and a reward \(r(x)\) that is fixed with respect to \(\theta\). RL optimizes the policy to increase expected reward; it does not, in this step, optimize the reward function itself.

\[ J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{x\sim q_\theta}[r(x)]. \] \[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta J &=\int r(x)\,\nabla_\theta q_\theta(x)\,dx\\ &=\mathbb E_{q_\theta}\!\left[r(x)\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta(x)\right]. \end{aligned} \] (1) The likelihood-ratio / REINFORCE identity

This is the REINFORCE structure. [1] We do not need \(\nabla_x r\): a scalar judgment is enough. This is useful for discrete tokens, environment interactions, and nondifferentiable verifiers. Subtracting an action-independent baseline leaves the expected gradient unchanged because \(\mathbb E_{q_\theta}[\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta]=0\). Outputs with higher reward relative to that baseline receive a positive log-probability weight; shared parameters still couple their updates.

3. OPD: a teacher supplies an implicit reward

Choose a sequence-level reverse-KL distillation objective:

\[ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{OPD}}(\theta) =\mathrm{KL}(q_\theta\|p_T) =\int q_\theta(x)\log\frac{q_\theta(x)}{p_T(x)}\,dx. \] \[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\mathrm{OPD}} &=\int \nabla_\theta q_\theta(x) \left(\log\frac{q_\theta(x)}{p_T(x)}+1\right)dx\\ &=\mathbb E_{q_\theta}\!\left[ (\log q_\theta-\log p_T)\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta\right]. \end{aligned} \] (2) The +1 integrates to zero: \(\nabla_\theta\int q_\theta\,dx=0\)

Negate the loss to align with RL's maximization convention:

\[ \boxed{\nabla_\theta(-\mathcal L_{\mathrm{OPD}}) =\mathbb E_{q_\theta}\!\left[ \underbrace{\log p_T(x)-\log q_\theta(x)}_{r_{\rm implicit}(x)} \nabla_\theta\log q_\theta(x)\right].} \] (3) A teacher–student log ratio is a policy-gradient reward

The reward asks a relative question: does the teacher assign this output more probability than the student does? A plausible output can still be overproduced by the student. The subtraction of \(\log q_\theta\) detects that. This sequence-level reverse-KL and policy-gradient connection is explicit in MiniLLM. [3]

The implicit reward depends on \(\theta\). Usually a parameter-dependent reward adds an extra derivative. Here that term is \(-\mathbb E_{q_\theta}[\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta]=0\). This cancellation justifies treating the computed log ratio as a constant weight in a sampled policy-gradient surrogate. It is a property of this expression, not a general rule to ignore reward derivatives.

Sequences need credit assignment

For a finite response \(y_{1:H}\), let \(h_k\) contain the prompt and preceding tokens. Define the per-token log ratio \(r_k\) and its reward-to-go \(R_k\):

\[ r_k=\log\frac{p_T(y_k\mid h_k)}{q_\theta(y_k\mid h_k)}, \qquad R_k=\sum_{j=k}^{H}r_j. \] \[ \nabla_\theta(-\mathcal L_{\mathrm{OPD}}) =\mathbb E\!\left[\sum_{k=1}^{H} (R_k-b(h_k))\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta(y_k\mid h_k)\right]. \] (4) Earlier actions can affect later distillation rewards

The causal reward-to-go follows by removing past rewards whose expected contribution is zero. Using only the immediate \(r_k\) generally loses the effect of an earlier token on later prefixes. MiniLLM's decomposition makes this future-reward term visible. [3] PPO adds a surrogate with old/new policy ratios and clipping on top of policy-gradient reasoning; clipping does not follow automatically from differentiating KL. [4]

“On-policy” specifies where data come from, not a unique loss OPD can use several divergences. GKD, for example, evaluates token-distribution losses on student-generated prefixes while stopping gradients through prefix sampling. [2] Its reverse-KL variant should not be identified with the full sequence gradient in (4): the latter also accounts for how parameters change which prefixes are visited. The exact equivalence here concerns sequence-level reverse KL, or a single fixed-context action distribution.

4. KL-regularized RL defines a target distribution

So far, distillation has acquired an RL interpretation. The connection also works in the other direction. Let \(p_{\rm ref}\) be a fixed reference, \(\beta>0\), and consider

\[ J_\beta(\theta)=\mathbb E_{q_\theta}[r(x)] -\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(q_\theta\|p_{\rm ref}). \] \[ p^*(x)=\frac{p_{\rm ref}(x)e^{r(x)/\beta}}{Z}, \qquad Z=\int p_{\rm ref}(x)e^{r(x)/\beta}\,dx. \] (5) Assume \(0<Z<\infty\)

The reference supplies a starting distribution; exponentiated reward tilts it toward preferred outputs. Substitute \(\log p^*=\log p_{\rm ref}+r/\beta-\log Z\) into reverse KL:

\[ \begin{aligned} \beta\,\mathrm{KL}(q_\theta\|p^*) &=\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(q_\theta\|p_{\rm ref}) -\mathbb E_{q_\theta}[r]+\beta\log Z,\\[2pt] \boxed{J_\beta(\theta)} &\boxed{=-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(q_\theta\|p^*)+\beta\log Z.} \end{aligned} \] (6) An exact objective identity

Because \(Z\) is independent of the student parameters, the two optimization problems are equivalent. If the distillation teacher is \(p_T=p^*\), reverse-KL OPD optimizes the same objective, up to scale and a constant. A capacity-limited student finds the best available reverse-KL fit; it need not reach \(p^*\).

This reward–optimal-policy transformation also underlies DPO's derivation. [5] The identity alone does not give DPO's preference-pair loss, which additionally uses a preference model. Nor does an arbitrary teacher necessarily equal the optimum of a particular externally chosen reward.

Two boundaries of the equivalence: plain RL and general environments

Plain expected-reward maximization lacks the entropy/reference term in (6). For a finite action space, adding entropy corresponds to using a uniform reference. In a continuous unbounded space, a “uniform reference everywhere” is not a normalized distribution. We should therefore say KL-regularized RL when claiming this exact equivalence.

The algebra also holds for trajectory distributions with a common fixed environment. However, the reward-tilted trajectory distribution need not be realizable by a policy with those same stochastic dynamics. The objective identity remains true over feasible policies; the unconstrained optimum need not be attainable. For a fixed prompt and autoregressive text, we can simply treat the whole response as \(x\).

5. From rewarding samples to moving samples

Now suppose the sample is continuous and generated by a differentiable map:

\[ x=G_\theta(z),\qquad z\sim p_z, \qquad J_\theta(z)=\frac{\partial G_\theta(z)}{\partial\theta}. \]

The latent distribution \(p_z\) is fixed. Instead of differentiating the probability of a sampled output, we can hold \(z\) fixed and differentiate the output itself. Reparameterize the same reverse KL, with \(p\) fixed:

\[ \mathcal L(\theta)=\mathbb E_z\!\left[ \log q_\theta(G_\theta(z))-\log p(G_\theta(z))\right]. \] \[ \nabla_\theta\mathcal L =\mathbb E_z\!\left[J_\theta^{\mathsf T}(s_q-s_p)\right] +\underbrace{\mathbb E_{x\sim q_\theta}\!\left[ \left.\partial_\theta\log q_\theta(x)\right|_x\right]}_{0}. \] (7) Separate movement through x from explicit density dependence

Again, normalization cancels the explicit density-parameter derivative in expectation. What remains is the score difference, pulled back through the generator Jacobian:

\[ \boxed{\nabla_\theta(-\mathcal L) =\mathbb E_z\!\left[J_\theta^{\mathsf T}(s_p-s_q)\right].} \] \[ \boxed{\nabla_x\underbrace{(\log p-\log q_\theta)}_{\text{implicit reward}} =\underbrace{s_p-s_q}_{\text{sample-space direction}}.} \] (8) The bridge from OPD to score distillation

This answers the opening question. The log ratio grades an output; its spatial derivative says how that grade changes when the output moves. With both estimators available and the assumptions above satisfied, (3) and (8) have the same expected parameter gradient. Their Monte Carlo estimates, variance, and computational requirements differ. The word “score” also changes meaning across communities: \(\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta\) in REINFORCE is a parameter score; \(\nabla_x\log q_\theta\) in diffusion is a density score in sample space.

A Gaussian check: the two estimators really give the same gradient

Let \(q_\mu=\mathcal N(\mu,1)\), \(p=\mathcal N(m,1)\), and \(x=\mu+z\), with \(z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\). The exact loss is \(\mathcal L=\tfrac12(\mu-m)^2\), so its negative gradient is \(m-\mu\).

\[ r_\mu(x)=(m-\mu)x+\tfrac12(\mu^2-m^2), \qquad \partial_\mu\log q_\mu(x)=x-\mu. \] \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Likelihood ratio:}\quad &\mathbb E[r_\mu(x)(x-\mu)]=m-\mu,\\ \text{Pathwise:}\quad &\mathbb E[\underbrace{\partial_\mu x}_{1} \underbrace{(s_p-s_q)}_{m-\mu}]=m-\mu. \end{aligned} \]

For Figure 2, both give \(+2\). The pathwise estimator happens to have zero variance in this mean-only example; that is a property of this Gaussian calculation, not a universal variance guarantee.

The score difference is a direction in output space. An actual generator update is \(\Delta\theta\propto\mathbb E[J_\theta^{\mathsf T}(s_p-s_q)]\), so shared parameters couple the movement of different outputs. It is not generally identical to moving every output independently along a free particle flow. The previous flow note develops that distinction.

6. VSD and DMD: make the comparison at noise scales

The clean-space derivation leaves a practical problem. A neural generator often has no tractable likelihood, and its clean output distribution can be concentrated on a thin set. A diffusion teacher instead exposes scores at positive noise levels. We therefore compare the distributions after applying the same forward noising kernel.

\[ x_t=\alpha_tG_\theta(z)+\sigma_t\epsilon, \qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I), \] \[ \begin{aligned} q_{\theta,t}(x_t)&=\int K_t(x_t\mid x_0)q_\theta(x_0)\,dx_0,\\ p_{T,t}(x_t)&=\int K_t(x_t\mid x_0)p_T(x_0)\,dx_0. \end{aligned} \] \[ \boxed{\mathcal L_{\rm noisy}(\theta) =\mathbb E_{t\sim\rho}\!\left[ w(t)\,\mathrm{KL}(q_{\theta,t}\|p_{T,t})\right].} \] (9) Fixed \(\rho\), nonnegative \(w(t)\), and \(\sigma_t>0\) at sampled noise levels

These are noisy marginals of the final output distribution. We integrate out the clean image and noise; a marginal describes the resulting population at a chosen noise level. It need not describe an intermediate state visited by the student's actual sampler. Even a one-step student can be evaluated at many such levels.

Apply (7) separately at each \(t\). Since \(\partial_\theta x_t=\alpha_tJ_\theta\),

\[ \boxed{\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\rm noisy} =\mathbb E_{t,z,\epsilon}\!\left[ w(t)\alpha_tJ_\theta^{\mathsf T} \big(s_{q,t}(x_t)-s_{T,t}(x_t)\big)\right].} \] (10) Descent pushes in the teacher-minus-student score direction

This is the ideal noisy-marginal two-score gradient. Diff-Instruct explicitly formulates an integral KL of this kind. [7] The original DMD derives a clean-distribution score gradient and implements it through noisy scores, with practical weighting and an additional regression term. [8] Equation (9) captures the shared mathematical core with fixed time weights; it is not a verbatim specification of every DMD training loss.

Where VSD fits

VSD's original setting is text-to-3D. It optimizes a distribution \(\mu\) over scene parameters, represented by particles. For a camera \(c\), rendering \(x_0=R(\vartheta,c)\), \(\vartheta\sim\mu\), induces an image distribution \(q_t^\mu(\cdot\mid c)\). The objective matches these noisy rendered-image distributions to a diffusion prior, averaging over noise and cameras; each particle receives the score difference through its renderer Jacobian. [6]

DMD instead trains a shared image generator \(G_\theta\). [8] Thus VSD and DMD share the noisy-distribution / two-score structure, but the variables being optimized and their parameterization differ. A renderer can only produce renderable views; a compact generator can only represent its model family. Matching a target remains subject to those constraints.

Why there is a fake-score network

The teacher estimates \(s_{T,t}\). The other score must describe the current student's population. Train an auxiliary denoiser on newly generated images with the generator held fixed:

\[ \min_\phi\;\mathbb E_{t,z,\epsilon} \left[\lambda(t)\|\epsilon_\phi(x_t,t)-\epsilon\|^2\right]. \] \[ \epsilon_\phi^*(x_t,t)=\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_t,t], \qquad s_{q,t}(x_t)=-\frac{\epsilon_\phi^*(x_t,t)}{\sigma_t}. \] (11) Positive denoising weights on the trained noise levels

Substituting exact noise predictors into (10) gives a generator-descent bracket proportional to \((\epsilon_T-\epsilon_\phi)/\sigma_t\), with the \(w(t)\alpha_t\) factor retained. In practice, the learned fake score only approximates the optimum and can lag behind a moving student. This estimator is part of the VSD / Diff-Instruct / DMD connection. [6] [7] [8]

The same entropy term appears in two forms \(-\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)=\mathbb E_q[\log p]+H(q)\). In the likelihood-ratio view, entropy contributes the \(-\log q\) part of the implicit reward. In the pathwise view, it contributes \(-s_q\). Removing the student term loses this distributional correction: maximizing teacher log likelihood alone is a different objective.

7. What survives in an actual training loop?

The exact identities are useful because they tell us where to stop gradients and where an implementation departs from its ideal objective.

A sampled reverse-KL policy-gradient surrogate

# x is sampled from the current student; log probabilities sum over tokens.
# Teacher probabilities are evaluated on that same sampled sequence.
logq = student_log_prob(x)
logp = teacher_log_prob(x)
reward = (logp - logq).detach()
loss_pg = -(reward * logq).mean()
loss_pg.backward()

This illustrates the whole-sequence estimator in (3). Reward-to-go and valid baselines can reduce variance. The log-ratio weight is detached because its role here is to implement an already-derived gradient. This instruction does not mean detaching all student probabilities inside an analytically summed token KL; differentiating that explicit KL is a different implementation.

A two-score generator surrogate

# Generator update: both score estimates are evaluated, then detached.
x0 = generator(z)
xt = alpha * x0 + sigma * noise
with no_grad():
    direction = w * (fake_score(xt, t) - teacher_score(xt, t))
loss_dm = (direction * xt).flatten(1).sum(1).mean()
loss_dm.backward()  # d xt / d theta supplies alpha * the generator Jacobian

Here time scalars broadcast per sample, and both functions return density scores. The score difference is held fixed during the generator backward pass, while the path through \(x_t\) stays differentiable. In a separate denoising update, generated images are detached and the fake-score model is trained. These surrogate scalar values are not estimates of the KL itself; their gradients implement the desired updates.

For a fixed distribution and exact scores, the identities above are exact. A learned or stale fake score introduces gradient error. Classifier-free guidance supplies a modified teacher score field, which need not be the forward-noised marginals of the teacher sampler's clean output law. Sample-dependent gradient normalization also need not equal the gradient of (9) with a fixed \(w(t)\). Such choices should be described as choices in the applied update. The method-by-method note treats these practical distinctions in more detail.

The full original DMD objective additionally includes paired regression. [8] DMD2 removes that requirement, improves fake-score tracking with a two-time-scale update scheme, and introduces an adversarial loss using real data. [9] These modifications matter: “DMD is reverse KL plus reparameterization” is a useful description of its distribution-matching core, not its complete recipe.

8. A compact map of the connection

ViewWhat defines improvement?How do we obtain an update?
RL / policy gradientExpected external rewardA scalar return weights \(\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta\)
KL-regularized RLReverse KL to \(p^*\propto p_{\rm ref}e^{r/\beta}\)Policy gradient, or another valid optimizer
Reverse-KL OPDReverse KL to a fixed teacherThe log ratio acts as an implicit reward; full sequences require future credit

The connection can now be stated precisely: RL optimizes a policy for expected reward. Reverse-KL OPD uses a teacher–student log ratio as an implicit reward. The noisy-marginal core of VSD/DMD applies distribution matching at selected noise levels and uses reparameterization to turn that log ratio into a score difference.

What made this connection satisfying was that the “reward” and the “direction” had been next to each other all along:

\[ \underbrace{\log p-\log q_\theta}_{\text{how much to favor an output}} \quad\xrightarrow{\quad\nabla_x\quad}\quad \underbrace{s_p-s_q}_{\text{which way to move that output}}. \]

When reading a new method, I can now ask whether it changed the target, the divergence, the sampling distribution, or the gradient estimator. A new-looking training loop often becomes much easier to understand once those choices are visible.
